Abstract

We introduce ECHO, a transformer–operator framework for generating million- point PDE trajectories. While existing neural operators (NOs) have shown promise for solving partial differential equations, they remain limited in practice due to poor scalability on dense grids, error accumulation during dynamic unrolling, and task-specific design. ECHO addresses these challenges through three key innovations. (i) It employs a hierarchical convolutional encode–decode architecture that achieves a 100× spatio-temporal compression while preserving fidelity on mesh points. (ii) It incorporates a training and adaptation strategy that enables high-resolution PDE solution generation from sparse input grids. (iii) It adopts a generative modeling paradigm that learns complete trajectory segments, mitigating long-horizon error drift. The training strategy decouples representation learning from downstream task supervision, allowing the model to tackle multiple tasks such as trajectory generation, forward and inverse problems, and interpolation. The generative model further supports both conditional and unconditional generation. We demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on million-point simulations across diverse PDE systems featuring complex geometries, high-frequency dynamics, and long-term horizons.

Our recipe to design a neural operator for PDEs

  • Progressive hierarchical spatio-temporal compression. Most encoders downsample only spatial dimensions and perform compression in a single step, leading to poor scalability and information loss. We introduce a hierarchical encoder–decoder that progressively compresses both space and time, yielding compact yet faithful spatio-temporal representations.
  • Trajectory-level generation. Autoregressive prediction remains the dominant paradigm, but suffers from error drift. We instead generate complete trajectory segments, enforcing horizon-wide consistency, mitigating error drift, and improving long- term stability.
  • Generative modeling in compressed latent space. We adopt a stochastic formulation that models distributions over full trajectories rather than deterministic predictions, enabling robustness to partial observations and uncertainty.

The figure below illustrates the benefits of principles (i)–(iii): (left) our spatio-temporal encoder achieves a compression ratio versus relative L2 error that is markedly superior to state-of-the-art baselines enabling large scale applications; (center) its trajectory-generation procedure is far less prone to error accumulation, enabling long-horizon forecasts; and (right) the generative modeling paradigm outperforms deterministic alternatives.


The ECHO framework

We instantiate these principles in ECHO, a transformer-based neural operator for large-scale PDE modeling. By combining hierarchical compression with trajectory-level generative modeling, ECHO enables efficient learning and inference on million-point systems defined on arbitrary domains, where most SOTA neural operators fail. Critical to this capability is the efficient spatio-temporal representa- tion. Figure 1 demonstrates the impact of each component: (left) significantly improved accuracy at high compression ratios as an enabler of large-scale applications, (center) a trajectory generation mechanism that reduces long-horizon error accumulation, and (right) a generative modeling paradigm that offers consistent gains over deterministic baselines. ECHO achieves state-of-the-art performance on large-scale 2D and 3D PDE benchmarks, scaling to simulations with up to 20 million points, where existing methods fail. In addition, we introduce a staged training strategy that enables high-fidelity learning under memory constraints and a unified formulation that supports forward prediction, backward prediction, interpo- lation, and long-horizon forecasting within a single model. This enables us to address a variety of problems in a zero-shot setting.

Study of the process & multi tasking Capabilities

At inference, ECHO addresses multiple tasks in a zero-shot manner. The comparison is performed on regular grids, where state-of-the-art baselines admit particularly efficient imple- mentations. We compare against neural solvers trained for time-stepping, the standard approach for time-dependent PDEs, on forward and backward prediction. We call backward prediction the generation of the earlier part of a trajectory from its last observed frames. This temporal task differs from classical PDE inverse problems, which recover unknown coefficients, forcing terms or initial conditions from observations of the solution. Since the baselines handle one task at a time, we train them separately for each direction, while ECHO handles both without retraining. For the forward (resp. backward) task, we use the first (resp. last) 4 timesteps as context and generate all remaining frames of the trajectory.

Despite operating in a highly compressed latent space, whereas all baselines except ENMA work directly in physical space without information loss, ECHO achieves the best overall accuracy, attaining the lowest error on seven of the eight dataset–task pairs. FNO performs poorly and fails to converge on two of the four datasets. The deterministic version of ECHO remains competitive across benchmarks, while the generative formulation outperforms it on all eight dataset–task pairs, highlighting the benefits of stochastic trajectory modeling.

ECHO successfully scales to 3D scenarios compared to existing baselines. Its full-trajectory generation design accumulates less errors than other baselines and makes it competitive on these datasets. In particular, we observe that FNO performs very well on first frame generation, but quickly diverges as the dynamic evolve, whereas ECHO provides consistent prediction along the entire trajectory.


Study of the Encoder & Decoder Architecture

Following ECHO’s success on regular grids (section 4.1), we now consider demanding irregular, dense meshes. We compare arbitrary-resolution neural operators that operate in a com- pressed latent space and can be queried at arbitrary resolutions. Unless stated otherwise, all methods use matched encoder–decoder compression rates. Each encoder-decoder is first trained and evaluated on reconstruction to assess physical information preservation. These experiments highlight ECHO’s consistent superiority. While graph and INR approaches fail to encode these complex dynamics, attention-based AROMA proves powerful on smaller datasets like Cylinder Flow but struggles scaling to Vorticity due to high memory costs. Conversely, the convolution-based CALM-PDE handles Vorticity better given its lighter footprint. Alongside ECHO, this shows that hierarchical convolutional architectures excel on large-scale data. Furthermore, operating in a spatio-temporally compressed latent space mitigates generation error accumulation. This is evident on Cylinder Flow: AROMA yields the best reconstruction, yet ECHO achieves higher forward task accuracy.


Stability on long-range generation

We further illustrate ECHO’s ability to generate long-range predictions beyond the training horizon on the Vorticity dataset, which exhibits turbulent regimes and the Shallow-Water for generation up to 4 times the training horizon. The Figure below illustrates that ECHO remains consistent in long-rollouts, even on turbulent dynamics such as the Vorticity PDE. On Shallow-Water, ECHO is trained on trajectories of length T = 40 time steps, and evaluated in an out-of-distribution regime up to 4T = 160. The moderate and monotonic degradation indicates that ECHO produces stable long-range forecasts even when extrapolating four times beyond its training horizon.


Probabilistic analysis

Single-sample errors do not assess the predictive distribution of a generative model. We therefore sample 16 trajectories per test case and report three standard probabilistic metrics, together with the relative L2 error of the ensemble mean. The CRPS, in its fair ensemble form, is a proper scoring rule that evaluates the full predictive distribution. The RMSCE (root mean squared calibration error) measures the gap between nominal and empirical coverage of the ensemble quantiles. The SSR (spread-skill ratio, with finite-ensemble correction) compares the ensemble spread with the error of the ensemble mean: a value of 1 indicates that the spread matches the realized error, and values below 1 indicate over-confident ensembles.

Forward prediction. On forward prediction (table 20), ECHO is well calibrated, with RMSCE between 9.0×10−2 and 1.7×10−1 and SSR between 0.67 and 0.93. ENMA is under-dispersed on every system, with SSR between 0.12 and 0.53, meaning that its ensembles are over-confident relative to their realized error. ENMA obtains a slightly lower CRPS on Rayleigh-Bénard; on the three other systems, ECHO is better on all four metrics.

Backward prediction. Backward prediction can be ill-posed, since several earlier states may be consistent with the observed frames, which makes the calibration of the ensembles the relevant property. Backward performance of ECHO is close to its forward performance on all four systems, with CRPS increasing by 1% (Active Matter) to 25% (Rayleigh-Bénard). The backward ensembles are well calibrated, with RMSCE between 4.8×10−2 and 1.3×10−1 and SSR between 0.80 and 1.06: the predicted spread reflects the actual uncertainty of the reconstruction.


Additional Results

Our paper presents a comprehensive analysis of ECHO and its capabilities. We deeply study the architecture of ECHO and its performance on various benchmarks. To this end, we provide :

  • Out-of-distribution evaluation on the Vorticity equation: we evaluate the OOD capacities of ECHO on new unseen physical parameters.
  • Generative trajectory sampling in an unconditional setting on Shallow-Water, including Fréchet Physics Distance (FPD) and efficiency metrics.
  • Initial value problem (IVP) evaluation, where ECHO generates full trajectories from a single initial frame and PDE parameters.
  • Temporal interpolation tasks, in which ECHO reconstructs long missing segments between observed frames across four datasets.
  • Encoding sparse data / spatial interpolation, where only 50% or 10% of the input grid is observed and the full grid must be reconstructed, with 10% constituting a strong OOD sparsity level.
  • In addition, we provide a set of ablation studies analyzing:
    • the impact of hierarchical compression depth in the encoder on reconstruction accuracy;
    • the contribution of the refinement stage for full-resolution Vorticity reconstruction;
    • the robustness of the flow-matching generative process to the choice of ODE solver and number of integration steps at sampling time.
Please refer to the paper for these additional expriments and analysis.
